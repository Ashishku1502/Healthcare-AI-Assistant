# Parchi v1: decision memo
**The bet.** v1 is a WhatsApp-first receptionist companion plus a voice answering layer behind call forwarding (on busy / no answer). It answers the 53% of contacts that are "is the doctor in / wait" (44%) or fees, timings, address (9%), books visits only after the patient says yes, and hands every uncertain, medical, urgent or sensitive turn to a human through a one-tap "Needs attention" list on the receptionist's own phone. The receptionist's one-tap doctor status is the live source of truth; Parchi never says anything about the wait that the clinic has not set.
**Three things I am deliberately not building.** (1) A full appointment calendar or EMR that replaces the paper register: 26 of 40 clinics use a paper register, 68% of visits are walk-ins, and Parchi would be a second system nobody updates. (2) Symptom triage that tells a parent "come now" or "wait": the doctor is the one "who ends up in court". (3) A voice that hides that it is a machine.
## What I noticed in the evidence
- **Mean call is 70 s, median 48 s.** Cost follows total minutes, so the mean sets the budget: a median clinic gets about 1,145 calls and 1,335 minutes a month. Telephony alone is Rs 801 of the Rs 1,000 ceiling if every call is answered.
- **A phone number is not an identity** (call 3: the number belongs to another patient) **and not consent** (call 7: a reminder reached a number that said "no messages"). So identity and contact permission live per person, not per number.
- **The truthful wait is the product, and it hurts the person who must feed it.** Sunita says "aa rahe hain" to keep patients; 57% of sessions start 30+ minutes late. If she dislikes the status tap, the product starves. She is my sensor and must feel helped, not replaced.
- **Meera's third ask (a "proper calendar") contradicts her own data.** Paper registers, 68% walk-ins and a 31% no-show rate on booked visits mean the value is in live state and call coverage, not in a calendar.
- **Missed calls are the pain, not wrong answers:** about 15 missed calls in the morning rush, and a callback that arrives after the caller hung up (call 1: 5-minute reply, 20-second patience).
- **35% of callers are over 55 and will not press buttons; 12% speak Marathi or other.** Speech-first, no menus, and a Marathi evaluation set before launch.
- **Only 6 of 40 clinics will move their number**, so forwarding, not number porting, is the integration.
- **The pilot used humans, so it shows demand, not model performance.** I cannot know how safe the model is until I replay real calls. That is why the week-2 gate exists.
## The eight tensions
**1. Does the voice admit it is a machine?** Chosen: yes, in the first 5 seconds: "Namaste, main Parchi hoon, clinic ka AI sahayak. Boliye, kya madad chahiye?" Cost: some hang-ups. Reverse if an A/B test on 200+ calls shows disclosure raises hang-ups by more than 10 points AND the doctors accept in writing the legal exposure of non-disclosure. To Meera: "Patients forgive a machine faster than a lie, and the doctor is answerable for what is said in the clinic's name, so hiding it risks his licence. We will make it warm and fast instead, which is what stops hang-ups."
**2. The feverish child.** Line: Parchi gives no clinical judgement (no come-now/wait, no dose, no reassurance). It does two things: red-flag words go to "call 112 / nearest hospital" plus an immediate staff alert; everything else gets a doctor's-team callback with a promised time, plus the earliest slot if wanted. Accountable: Parchi and its safety lead for the routing and the words it uses; the doctor for any clinical answer. Call 2: no advice, callback within the clinic-set window, 112 for breathing trouble, drowsiness or seizure, earliest token offered, item in Needs attention. Call 5: "seene mein dard" anywhere in the call stops the routine booking, says 112 or hospital now, and raises an URGENT item for staff to call. Cost: mothers who wanted an answer get a callback instead (the "toh phone kyun uthaya" complaint). Reverse if doctors sign a triage protocol they own, and then only for that protocol.
**3. The truth about the wait.** Parchi states only what the receptionist or doctor set ("Doctor is about 50 minutes late") and says "the clinic has not confirmed" if the status is older than 90 minutes, while pinging staff. The clinic sets the content; Parchi refuses to assert something known to be false; the patient's interest is the floor I will not go below. Cost: Sunita loses her buffer and some patients may go across the road. Reverse if pilot data shows truthful waits raise no-arrivals more than they cut wasted trips; then I would add "earliest likely time" ranges, still not "he is on his way".
**4. Where live state comes from.** One tap on the receptionist's phone (Arrived / Late 30 / Late 60 / Not arrived), or the same words texted by WhatsApp from her number. Day one: she turns on forwarding, sets status once, and afterwards taps 4-6 times an hour: about 20-30 seconds an hour. The paper register stays. Reverse if fewer than 60% of clinics keep status fresh by week 3; then add a doctor-side WhatsApp reply and a "last updated" nudge.
**5. The economics of voice.** For a median clinic (1,145 calls, 1,335 minutes, 468 WhatsApp replies a month; formulas in the cost sheet): speech-to-speech about Rs 8,930; cascaded about Rs 3,322; both break the Rs 1,000 ceiling, and even telephony alone is Rs 801. Both fail, so I change the architecture and scope rather than the price (Meera lost the room at Rs 3,000 and Rs 4,000): forward only busy / no-answer calls (assumed 35%), play pre-generated audio for templated answers (assumed 50% of those minutes), and use the cascaded pipeline only for open turns: about Rs 818, leaving little room. Both assumptions are unverified; at 60% forwarded the cost is about Rs 1,320, so I would cap minutes or price heavy clinics. I will ask the founder the forwarded share.
**6. The receptionist.** Sensor first, user second, never competitor. Her taps are the live state; Parchi takes the missed-call load and the repetitive "is doctor in" calls. To the owner paying Rs 9,000: "You are not replacing Sunita. You are paying to stop the ~15 calls she misses in the rush and the questions that pull her away from the patient in front of her."
**7. Unsure, live.** Within 2 seconds, a pre-recorded filler: "Ek second, main dekh rahi hoon". Then one short yes/no question in his language ("Aap doctor sahab ke aane ke baare mein pooch rahe hain? Haan ya nahi"). After a second miss: "Main clinic staff ko aapka number de rahi hoon, woh abhi call karenge", with a staff transfer or callback item. Never a button menu. Reverse if the yes/no question lowers completion versus an immediate transfer.
**8. Local rules.** Parchi learns: opening hours, doctor-written queue rules (for example "elderly first"), who may be relayed. It refuses undisclosed queue-jumping for named people (Sharma ji's son gets his message relayed and a normal token) and lying about the doctor's availability (the pharma rep gets "the clinic will get back to you", never an invented "busy"). A rule enters through the owner's settings page, is versioned and audited, and applies only if the doctor (not the receptionist) approves it.
## Ships
- **Week 3:** WhatsApp bot, receptionist status taps, handoff list, safety policy v1 (the working slice in this submission).
- **Week 6:** call-forwarding voice for templated answers plus cascaded fallback in 6 pilot clinics; Hindi, Hinglish and Marathi evaluation set; reminders sender.
- **Week 10:** 40 clinics live, per-clinic minute caps, weekly safety review, owner rules page.
## Success metrics and risks
Metrics: calls answered without a human (target 60%), urgent-symptom recall (must be 100%), hang-up rate under 2 s silence, status-freshness (60%+), cost per clinic below Rs 1,000. Risks: red-flag words missed (mitigation: rules first, labelled replay, recall gate); forwarding share higher than assumed (mitigation: caps, pricing for heavy clinics); receptionist stops tapping (mitigation: WhatsApp text and nudges).
## What I do not know
The real forwarded share, Marathi volume per clinic, whether doctors will sign a triage protocol, how callers react to disclosure, and real audio accuracy. All are in the founder questions and the week-2 gate.
